01Industry & the Gulf

Emirates Extends A380 Heavy Maintenance Deal to 2030

Emirates has extended its A380 base maintenance agreement with Lufthansa Technik Philippines through 2030. The two have worked together since 2023, and more than 30 A380 checks have been completed for the airline so far. Lufthansa Technik describes the renewal as among the largest by value in its Philippine unit's history.

Base maintenance is the heavy end of the trade. The aircraft comes out of service for weeks, goes into a hangar, and is opened up, inspected and rebuilt — as opposed to line maintenance, the quick checks done at the gate between flights. Two dedicated A380 lines run in Manila, and Emirates will be launch customer at a new Lufthansa Technik facility at Clark International Airport once it opens.

Note where the work happens. Emirates flies the world's largest A380 fleet out of Dubai, but sends those aircraft to the Philippines for heavy checks.

30+ A380 checks completed for Emirates since 2023. Diagram: line vs base maintenance, and the routing of the heavy checks.

Why it matters here Hangar slot, capacity and skilled labour decide where an aircraft is maintained — not the airline's home airport. MRO is a global market, and the licence you hold is what lets you work in it.

02Technical

SWISS Brings Composite Repair In-House at Zurich

SWISS is building a composite repair shop at Zurich Airport, expected to be operational by the end of 2027. The airline says bringing this work in-house will increase its independence and flexibility, and cut its reliance on outside providers.

Composites — carbon-fibre reinforced plastics and honeycomb sandwich panels — now make up a large share of a modern airframe. They are lighter than aluminium and they do not corrode, but they fail differently. Damage is often invisible from outside: an impact can separate the internal layers or crush the core while the surface still looks intact.

Finding it means tap testing, ultrasonic or thermographic inspection rather than a visual check. Repairing it means bonding, curing and vacuum bagging rather than riveting on a patch — different skills, different tooling, a different mindset from sheet metal work.

2027 — year the Zurich shop is due to open. Diagram: barely visible impact damage in a sandwich panel, the reason composite inspection cannot be done by eye.

Why it matters here Composite repair is one of the sharpest skill shortages in the industry. The composites and NDT modules are not a side topic on the way to something else — they are the scarce capability employers are short of.

03Part 147 & licensing

Watchdog Questions Who May Sit the A&P Exam

The US Government Accountability Office has recommended that the FAA formally examine allowing students at institutions not certificated under Part 147 to sit the general written knowledge test for the Airframe & Powerplant certificate. The recommendation was reported on 1 September 2026.

Part 147 is the FAA rule governing Aviation Maintenance Technician Schools. A school holding a Part 147 certificate has had its curriculum, staff, tooling and facilities approved by the regulator, and its graduates qualify to sit the A&P exams on that basis. Everyone else must instead document the required hands-on experience and have it verified before they can test.

That one certificate decides who gets a straight route to the licence and who takes the long way round — which is why the GAO is asking whether the gate sits in the right place, at a moment when the industry says it cannot find enough technicians.

Two routes today, a third under review. Diagram: how a Part 147 certificate determines exam eligibility, and where the GAO recommendation would open a door.

Why it matters here This is a debate about the exact category of institution we are. Approval frameworks — FAA Part 147, EASA Part-147, GCAA CAR-147 — are what turn your coursework into exam eligibility rather than a certificate of attendance.
