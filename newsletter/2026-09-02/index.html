01Industry & supply chain

Retired A320s Are Being Turned Into Spare Parts

The aftermarket for used Airbus A320 parts is running hot. On 27 August, parts supplier Werner Aero confirmed its second A320-200 purchase of the month — aircraft MSN 2964 — which goes to the eCube facility at St Athan in Wales to be dismantled. Aviation Week's daily MRO log recorded further teardown deals in the same week, including a HEICO subsidiary, Prime Air, taking an ex-Spirit Airlines A320neo to Arizona for component disassembly.

When an aircraft is retired, usable components — landing gear, avionics boxes, actuators, cabin items — are removed, inspected, repaired and re-certified. They then re-enter service as USM: used serviceable material. USM is legal, traceable, and often the fastest route to a part when new production has long lead times.

Note how young some of these airframes are. Neo-generation aircraft being torn down this early tells you how tight the parts market has become.

Why it matters here Teardown, parts trading and certification paperwork are real MRO careers, not a sideline. Students should understand traceability, Form 1 / 8130-3 documentation and airworthiness release — that is what turns a removed part into a saleable one.

02Technical & regulatory

FAA Orders Repeat Wing Inspections on Boeing 787s

The FAA published Airworthiness Directive 2026-17-02 in the Federal Register on 27 August 2026, effective 1 October 2026. It applies to Boeing 787-8, 787-9 and 787-10 aircraft.

The unsafe condition traces back to build quality: shim gaps may have exceeded engineering allowances, and high pull-up forces used to close those gaps could start fatigue cracks at fastener holes. A shim is a thin filler placed between two structural parts so they mate without being forced together.

Operators must carry out repetitive ultrasonic (UT) and detailed visual (DET) inspections of the lower side-of-body splice plates, rear and front spar terminal fittings, lower chords and jack pads — and repair anything found. The FAA counts 17 US-registered aircraft affected, with cost estimated at up to $24,310 per aircraft per inspection cycle.

An Airworthiness Directive is not advice. It is a legally binding instruction; ignoring it makes the aircraft unairworthy.

Why it matters here This directive touches three things on our syllabus at once: non-destructive testing, primary structure and fatigue, and human factors in assembly. A manufacturing decision can become a maintenance task for the next twenty years.

03Careers & licensing

Watchdog Asks FAA to Widen Mechanic Exam Access

The US Government Accountability Office has recommended that the FAA formally examine letting students at non-certificated institutions sit the general written knowledge test for the Airframe & Powerplant (A&P) certificate. The recommendation was reported on 1 September 2026. At present that route is generally open to graduates of FAA-certificated maintenance schools, or to applicants who can document the required practical experience.

It lands against a clear demand picture. Aviation Week reported on 27 August that the Asia-Pacific region alone will need more than 300,000 new maintenance technicians over the next decade, citing Boeing's forecast that the region will account for over 40% of the world's new technicians through 2045. In the same week HAECO said a planned joint venture in Vietnam would create more than 1,000 jobs.

The debate is essentially about how many doors lead to a licence, and how quickly.
